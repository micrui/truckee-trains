Who built it, and who keeps it open

Chinese workers cut this railroad through Donner granite by hand, in winters that buried their camps, and nobody counted their dead. What Truckee did to those workers afterward is also part of the town's record. So is who does the mountain's work now.

The workforce

By early 1867 about ninety percent of the Central Pacific's construction force was Chinese, roughly eleven thousand men, most recruited from Guangdong province. They were hired at $26 a month against about $35 for white workers; by 1867 their pay had risen to $35, still below white wages, and unlike white workers they paid for their own food and board. In June 1867 as many as three thousand of them struck in the high Sierra for equal wages and shorter tunnel shifts; the railroad cut off their food supply and the strike ended after eight days without concessions.

Sources: Stanford Chinese Railroad Workers in North America Project · National Park Service

The mountain work

The Summit Tunnel at Donner Pass was drilled by hand through granite from four faces at once, progress measured in inches per shift, with nitroglycerin briefly and lethally in use. The winter of 1866–67 brought forty-four storms; crews lived and worked under the snow in tunnels dug between camp and portal, and avalanches carried workers away, some found only when the snow melted. No one recorded the dead carefully. Published estimates of Chinese deaths on the Central Pacific run from around fifty to more than a thousand; the range itself, and the fact that nobody counted, is the historical record. The tunnel opened November 30, 1867. In December 2024 the workers' camp beside it, Summit Camp, was designated a National Historic Landmark.

Sources: PBS American Experience · Stanford project (death toll discussion) · YubaNet, Dec. 2024

What Truckee did afterward

When the railroad was finished, many workers settled where the work had been. Truckee's Chinatown became one of the largest in the Sierra: in 1870, 407 of the town's 1,467 residents were Chinese. The town's response is documented. In 1876, white vigilantes raided woodcutters' cabins at Trout Creek, burned them, and shot the men fleeing, killing one; the one man tried for murder was acquitted in nine minutes, and the arson charges were dismissed. In 1878 Chinatown was burned and its residents pushed across the river. And in 1886, town leaders led by newspaperman Charles McGlashan organized a total economic boycott, firing Chinese workers and starving Chinese businesses of custom until the community left, within five weeks by one account and ten by another. Promoters named it the "Truckee Method" and campaigned it across California as the respectable way to expel a town's Chinese population.

Truckee's expulsion was one of scores across the West in those years, alongside the Rock Springs massacre and the Tacoma and Seattle expulsions, all following the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act. Nevada County designated the site of Truckee's Chinatown a historical landmark in 2023, and a memorial plaque was placed downtown in 2024.

Who keeps it open now

Keeping the line open through a Donner winter is continuous work. Union Pacific clears it from Roseville with flanger trains that run ahead of storms, spreaders, and two ex-Southern Pacific rotary snowplows held for the heaviest winters; enthusiast accounts describe round-the-clock shifts staged from Truckee during big storms. The railroad publishes no staffing figures for the mountain, so the operational detail in the public record is largely the work of the photographers and railfans who document it.
